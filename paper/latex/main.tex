\title{Agentic Lightweight Consensus for Resilient Monitoring and

\author[unisa]{Giuseppe Fenza}

\begin{abstract}
Modern data centres operate as tightly coupled cyber--physical systems
in which thermal, power, and workload signals must be reconciled in
real time to keep monitoring and control loops both responsive and
trustworthy. A growing body of work addresses coordination in sensor-rich
infrastructures through blockchain-oriented lightweight consensus, yet
the agreement problem that dominates data-centre operation is not
transaction settlement: it is the derivation of stable, attack-resilient
operational state from noisy, heterogeneous, and possibly deceptive
local reports. This paper proposes an agentic lightweight consensus
architecture that addresses this problem directly. The architecture
organises sensing, edge aggregation, zone verification, and
facility-level arbitration into a hierarchy of cooperating agents, and
grounds its consensus layer in the soft-consensus tradition of fuzzy
group decision making: sensor reports are scored for reliability,
compared through fuzzy preference relations, ordered by
quantifier-guided dominance, and aggregated with ordered weighted
averaging under a weak-majority linguistic quantifier. A
consensus-reaching control loop monitors agreement, proximity, and
temporal consistency, and applies targeted corrective feedback to
unreliable reporters. The control architecture is deliberately split
into two paths: a deterministic, per-tick fast path
(sensing\,$\rightarrow$\,fuzzy consensus\,$\rightarrow$\,verifier\,$\rightarrow$\,safe
action or fallback) that owns every safety-critical decision, and a
slow, event-triggered supervisory path in which a LangGraph-orchestrated
large language model planner reasons over consensus snapshots and
proposes occasional supervisory actions that must clear the same
non-bypassable verifier. We evaluate the architecture on a real-data
cyber--physical simulation driven by audited data-centre thermal,
workload, and anomaly-timing datasets, across $1{,}890$ scenarios
spanning seven deception/anomaly attacks. The results are deliberately
nuanced. No single aggregator dominates: simple averaging attains the
best global mean absolute error, while FPR--OWA wins under systematic
bias and, uniquely, supplies the confidence and anomaly signals on which
safe orchestration depends. The verifier proves essential---without it
every unsafe proposal is executed, with it none are---and self-refinement
increases the number of accepted safe actions at the price of latency.
Finally, intensive in-the-loop LLM supervision does not improve
estimation accuracy yet inflates loop latency past real-time deadlines,
whereas event-triggered supervision preserves estimation quality and
zero thermal-SLA violations while cutting the deadline-miss rate by
roughly $95\%$. The contribution is an architecture in which agentic
reasoning serves as sparse supervisory intelligence while the
safety-critical path stays lightweight, deterministic, fuzzy-consensus
driven, and verifier-guarded.
\end{abstract}

\begin{highlights}
\item A two-path architecture: a deterministic per-tick fuzzy-consensus
fast path and a sparse, event-triggered LLM supervisory path.
\item A lightweight FPR--OWA consensus operator whose value lies in
confidence and anomaly diagnostics, not in dominating MAE.
\item Across $1{,}890$ real-data scenarios no aggregator dominates;
redundancy and attack type, not the operator, drive accuracy.
\item A non-bypassable deterministic verifier reduces executed unsafe
actions to zero; without it, all unsafe proposals execute.
\item Event-triggered LLM supervision preserves accuracy and SLA safety
while cutting deadline misses by $\approx 95\%$ versus intensive use.
\end{highlights}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Modern data centres are cyber--physical systems (CPS) in which
computational workload, electrical power, thermal conditions, and
physical sensing evolve together under tight feedback. Production
telemetry studies show that workload, energy, and temperature signals
are strongly coupled and vary across spatial and temporal scales that
no single monitoring point can capture~\cite{ilager2023}. Monitoring in
this setting is therefore not a passive observability concern but part
of the operational control loop: local observations must be interpreted
quickly enough to support timely reaction, yet they must also be
reconciled across racks, zones, and the whole facility whenever they
conflict, drift, or imply facility-level trade-offs. Hierarchical and
edge-assisted monitoring platforms have demonstrated that layered
collection and local pre-processing are practical in internet data
centres~\cite{jiang2019,yan2021}, and autonomic multi-agent management
has long shown that data-centre decisions can be decomposed into
interacting local and global decision makers~\cite{das2008,tesauro2004}.
More recently, learning-based controllers have delivered substantial
energy savings in cooling management, while exposing how brittle
unconstrained data-driven actuation can be when thermal safety is at
stake~\cite{wang2024tcps,ran2023event}.

What remains underdeveloped between these layers is the
\emph{agreement} layer. The operational question a data-centre CPS must
answer at every decision round is which local reports to trust, how to
fuse them into a stable zone-level state, and what to do when reports
disagree---because of sensor noise, communication faults, or deliberate
deception. False-data-injection attacks against sensing channels are a
well-studied threat to CPS state estimation and can be crafted to
remain stealthy while degrading control quality~\cite{padhan2022}.
Lightweight approximate agreement shows that bounded local disagreement
can be achieved on small devices under lossy
communication~\cite{zhang2021approx}, but it offers no principled
account of \emph{graded} trust in individual reporters, nor a process
view of how agreement should be monitored and repaired over time.

A prominent research line approaches coordination in sensor-rich
infrastructures through blockchain, designing lightweight and
hierarchical consensus mechanisms that fit constrained
devices~\cite{ramachandran2026,haque2024,khan2018,reyna2018,novo2018,
pan2019edgechain,tuli2019fogbus,qu2021pofl}. This line answers a
different question: ledger consensus---Byzantine fault
tolerant~\cite{castro1999} or authority-based---agrees on the
\emph{order and integrity of transactions}, whereas data-centre
operation requires agreement on the \emph{current physical state} under
noise and deception, at rates and energy budgets where ledger
replication is unnecessary. We therefore treat our work not as a
competitor but as an alternative coordination paradigm, with blockchain
a complementary instrument for audit or settlement
(Section~\ref{sec:positioning}).

Two mature research traditions make such an alternative concrete. The
first is the theory of \emph{soft consensus} in fuzzy group decision
making (GDM), developed by Kacprzyk, Herrera-Viedma, and collaborators:
agreement among experts is treated as a graded, monitored process,
measured through consensus and proximity degrees, guided by linguistic
quantifiers such as ``most''~\cite{zadeh1983,yager1988,kacprzyk1986,
kacprzyk1988,kacprzyk1992,herrera1996,herreraviedma2002}, constrained by
consistency control~\cite{herreraviedma2007}, and repaired through
feedback that targets the participants most responsible for
disagreement~\cite{cabrerizo2009,cabrerizo2010soft,cabrerizo2010kbs,
herreraviedma2014}. This machinery---born for human experts---transfers
naturally to sensor agents, whose reports are likewise graded,
conflicting, and unequally reliable. The second tradition is
\emph{agentic AI}: large language model (LLM) agents that interleave
reasoning and acting~\cite{yao2023react}, refine their outputs through
verbal self-correction~\cite{shinn2023reflexion}, and can be organised
into role-specialised multi-agent systems~\cite{wang2024agents}. Agentic
orchestration brings flexible, explainable planning to CPS control, but
it also imports a new trust problem: an LLM planner can propose unsafe
actions. Our position is that LLM autonomy in a CPS must be bounded by
a deterministic, non-bypassable verification gate, with the consensus
layer supplying the validated evidence on which both planner and
verifier operate.

This paper integrates the two traditions into a single architecture for
data-centre CPS monitoring and control, and evaluates it on a real-data
cyber--physical simulation. We address four research questions, each
answered directly by the campaign of Section~\ref{sec:results}:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ1 (estimation).} How do the lightweight fuzzy-consensus
operator (FPR--OWA) and standard robust aggregators compare for
zone-level thermal state estimation under deception attacks, and what
governs estimation quality? We ask which method wins \emph{where},
rather than assuming any single operator dominates.
\item \textbf{RQ2 (diagnostic value).} Beyond point-estimate error, what
distinctive role does FPR--OWA play through the confidence and anomaly
signals it produces, and why are these signals---rather than a lower
MAE---the reason to retain it in a safety-oriented architecture?
\item \textbf{RQ3 (safety enforcement).} Does a non-bypassable
deterministic verifier eliminate the execution of unsafe LLM-proposed
actions, and what does self-refinement add in terms of accepted safe
actions versus computational cost?
\item \textbf{RQ4 (real-time feasibility).} How does the rate of LLM
supervision affect estimation accuracy, loop latency, and deadline
satisfaction, and can event-triggered supervision retain accuracy and
SLA safety while restoring real-time responsiveness?
\end{itemize}

We deliberately separate three concerns that the literature often
conflates: \emph{estimation accuracy} (RQ1--RQ2), \emph{safety
enforcement} (RQ3), and \emph{operational latency} (RQ4). The central
finding is that they are best served by different mechanisms---robust
fuzzy consensus for the first, a deterministic verifier for the second,
and sparse event-triggered LLM supervision for the third---rather than
by placing a single powerful reasoner in the loop.

The contributions are fourfold:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{C1: Architecture.} A blockchain-independent, hierarchical
agentic architecture for data-centre CPS sensing and control, in which
sensing agents, edge consensus agents, zone verifiers, an LLM planner,
and a deterministic safety verifier cooperate under explicit
trust-oriented design principles (Section~\ref{sec:architecture}).
\item \textbf{C2: Consensus methodology.} A lightweight consensus
operator (FPR--OWA) that scores reporter reliability, builds a fuzzy
preference relation over reporters, orders them by quantifier-guided
dominance, and aggregates with OWA weights induced by a weak-majority
linguistic quantifier, together with a zone-level consensus-reaching
loop with proximity, temporal consistency, and adaptive feedback rooted
in the Herrera-Viedma soft-consensus tradition
(Section~\ref{sec:methodology}).
\item \textbf{C3: Verifier-gated agentic control.} A control
methodology in which every LLM-proposed action must pass a
deterministic verifier enforcing freshness, confidence, actuator, and
thermal-safety constraints, with Reflexion-style refinement cycles and
complete execution traceability
(Section~\ref{sec:agentic}).
\item \textbf{C4: Empirical evidence.} A real-data evaluation over
$1{,}890$ scenarios and two real-time supervision regimes that
disentangles estimation accuracy, safety enforcement, and latency, and
that supports a measured rather than promotional account of when fuzzy
consensus, the verifier, and LLM supervision each help
(Sections~\ref{sec:setup}--\ref{sec:results}).
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work and states the research
gap. Section~\ref{sec:architecture} presents the architecture, the
system model, and the fault model. Section~\ref{sec:methodology}
formalises the consensus methodology, and Section~\ref{sec:agentic} the
verifier-gated agentic control loop. Section~\ref{sec:setup} describes
the experimental setup, Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the results,
and Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses implications and threats to
validity. Section~\ref{sec:conclusions} concludes.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Four research lines converge on this work: consensus for blockchain--IoT
integration, hierarchical monitoring and multi-agent management of data
centres, soft consensus in fuzzy group decision making, and LLM-based
agentic systems. They have largely progressed independently; we review
each and then state the gap.

\subsection{Consensus in Blockchain--IoT Integration}
\label{sec:related_blockchain}

Blockchain--IoT integration has produced a rich catalogue of consensus
mechanisms for constrained devices: authority-based validation,
edge-hosted ledgers, hybrid on-chain/off-chain patterns, and
energy-recycling schemes~\cite{khan2018,reyna2018,novo2018,
pan2019edgechain,tuli2019fogbus,qu2021pofl,haque2024}. Closest to our
starting point, Ramachandran's three-tier hierarchical framework matches
device classes to consensus roles, with district PBFT~\cite{castro1999}
under city-wide proof of authority~\cite{ramachandran2026}. All share one
object of agreement---the ledger---and guarantee transaction ordering,
immutability, and quorum bounds; the problem here, estimating physical
state from graded, partially deceptive reports at every control round,
lies outside that object, which is why we treat ledger consensus as
complement rather than baseline (Section~\ref{sec:positioning}). In the
non-ledger space, Zhang et al.\ design approximate consensus for small
devices in lossy networks~\cite{zhang2021approx}; we share that
lightweight spirit but add the treatment of reporter trust it lacks.

\subsection{Data-Centre Monitoring, Multi-Agent Management, and Safe
Control}
\label{sec:related_dc}

Hierarchical sensing is established practice in data centres. Jiang et
al.\ partition an internet data centre into logical grids whose edge
nodes pre-process environmental and host telemetry before
escalation~\cite{jiang2019}; Yan et al.\ combine edge computing,
wireless sensing, and IPMI-derived measurements for environmental
monitoring~\cite{yan2021}. On the management side, the Unity system
decomposes power and performance management into interacting
agents~\cite{das2008}, within the broader autonomic-computing programme
of decentralised, self-managing elements~\cite{tesauro2004}. Multi-layer
IoT/edge coordination through compact summaries rather than raw-state
collection is likewise well supported~\cite{ogino2018,nezami2021}.
Production-trace characterisation confirms the coupled dynamics of
workload, energy, and temperature that such hierarchies must
track~\cite{ilager2023}.

On the actuation side, deep reinforcement learning has reduced cooling
energy substantially, but unconstrained exploration violates thermal
safety; physics-guided safe RL mitigates this by rectifying recommended
actions against a learned thermal model~\cite{wang2024tcps}, and
event-driven formulations reduce unnecessary control
operations~\cite{ran2023event}. These results motivate two design
commitments of our architecture: control proposals must be validated
against explicit safety constraints before actuation, and the state
that feeds control must itself be validated against noise and
deception~\cite{padhan2022}.

\subsection{Soft Consensus in Fuzzy Group Decision Making}
\label{sec:related_gdm}

The consensus layer of this paper draws on a four-decade research
programme on consensus reaching in fuzzy GDM. Its foundations are the
calculus of linguistically quantified propositions~\cite{zadeh1983},
the OWA operator as the canonical implementation of relative
quantifiers such as ``most''~\cite{yager1988}, and the notion of a
\emph{soft} degree of consensus---most of the important individuals
agreeing on most of the relevant options---introduced by Kacprzyk and
colleagues~\cite{kacprzyk1986,kacprzyk1988,kacprzyk1992}. Herrera et
al.\ brought this view to linguistic assessments~\cite{herrera1996},
and Bordogna et al.\ modelled linguistic consensus through OWA
aggregation~\cite{bordogna1997}. Fuzzy preference relations became the
standard representation of graded pairwise judgements, with integration
results across preference formats~\cite{chiclana1998} and consensus
models that operate across heterogeneous
structures~\cite{herreraviedma2002}.

Three later developments matter most here. First, consensus models were
equipped with \emph{guidance}: consensus degrees and proximity measures
computed at several representation levels drive automatic feedback that
tells specific participants how to revise specific
assessments~\cite{herreraviedma2002,cabrerizo2009}. Second,
\emph{consistency control} was combined with consensus so that
agreement is not reached at the price of self-contradictory
preferences, including under incomplete
information~\cite{herreraviedma2007,cabrerizo2010kbs}. Third, the field
produced systematic analyses and reviews---of soft consensus models in
fuzzy environments~\cite{herreraviedma2014,cabrerizo2010soft,
cabrerizo2015}, of consensus measures~\cite{delmoral2018}, of
minimum-adjustment feedback mechanisms~\cite{zhang2020feedback}, and of
dynamic consensus and opinion dynamics in networked
settings~\cite{hassani2022}---together with operator-level tools such
as type-1 OWA for multi-granular linguistic
contexts~\cite{mata2014} and methodologies for unbalanced linguistic
term sets~\cite{herrera2008unbalanced}. Applications include
collaborative knowledge engineering under linguistic fuzzy
consensus~\cite{demaio2017}.

This tradition treats consensus as a monitored \emph{process} over
unequally reliable participants---exactly the structure of a zone of
sensor agents under deception. Its transfer to machine-to-machine
settings, however, remains largely unexplored: consensus-reaching
models assume human experts revising opinions, whereas sensor agents
require automated analogues of reliability, preference, dominance, and
feedback. Constructing those analogues is the methodological core of
this paper.

\subsection{LLM-Based Agents and Trustworthy Agentic Orchestration}
\label{sec:related_llm}

LLM-based agents couple language-model reasoning with tool use and
environment interaction~\cite{wang2024agents}. ReAct interleaves
explicit reasoning traces with actions, improving groundedness and
auditability~\cite{yao2023react}; Reflexion adds verbal reinforcement,
letting agents revise behaviour from feedback without weight
updates~\cite{shinn2023reflexion}. Reasoning-quality mechanisms such as
chain-of-thought prompting~\cite{wei2022cot} and self-consistency over
sampled reasoning paths~\cite{wang2023selfconsistency} establish the
sampling-and-agreement intuition that softer, fuzzy notions of consensus
generalise. In safety-critical domains, the emerging consensus is that
reasoning traces alone do not establish trustworthiness; explicit
verification stages and human-auditable evidence are required. We adopt
this position architecturally: the LLM planner is a proposer, never an
executor, and a deterministic verifier owns the actuation decision.

\subsection{Research Gap and Positioning}
\label{sec:positioning}

The literature thus supplies hierarchical sensing~\cite{jiang2019,
yan2021}, multi-agent data-centre management~\cite{das2008,tesauro2004},
lightweight approximate agreement~\cite{zhang2021approx}, a mature
theory of monitored, feedback-driven consensus~\cite{herreraviedma2014},
and agentic orchestration patterns~\cite{yao2023react,shinn2023reflexion}.
No existing work, to the best of our knowledge, integrates these
ingredients into a single blockchain-independent architecture in which
(i) the consensus object is operational state rather than a ledger,
(ii) reporter trust is modelled through fuzzy preference relations and
quantifier-guided dominance, (iii) consensus is monitored and repaired
as a process, and (iv) LLM-driven control is gated by deterministic
verification. Table~\ref{tab:positioning} summarises the qualitative
positioning of this proposal with respect to blockchain--IoT consensus;
the comparison is conceptual by design, since the two paradigms answer
different agreement problems.

\section{Architecture and System Model}
\label{sec:architecture}

This section presents the proposed architecture, the agents it
comprises, and the system and fault models under which it operates.

\subsection{Design Principles}
\label{sec:principles}

Five principles drive the design.

\begin{description}
\item[P1 --- Consensus on state, not on transactions.] The agreement
mechanism validates operational state at every decision round; it
maintains no ledger and requires no global ordering of events.
\item[P2 --- Lightweight, quantifier-guided aggregation.] Agreement is
computed locally on zone-sized groups of reports with closed-form
operators (medians, fuzzy preference relations, OWA weights), so that
the consensus step remains compatible with edge-class hardware.
\item[P3 --- Consensus as a monitored process.] Following the
soft-consensus tradition~\cite{herreraviedma2014}, agreement is measured
(consensus degree, proximity, consistency) and repaired (targeted
feedback) rather than assumed after a single aggregation step.
\item[P4 --- Bounded agentic autonomy.] LLM-based planning is a
proposal mechanism. A deterministic verifier owns the decision to
actuate; no proposed action can bypass it.
\item[P5 --- Traceability by construction.] Every consensus snapshot,
planner proposal, verifier decision, and refinement cycle is logged in
structured, replayable execution traces.
\end{description}

\subsection{Layered Architecture and Agent Roles}
\label{sec:layers}

Figure~\ref{fig:architecture} depicts the architecture as two
interacting paths. The \emph{fast path} is deterministic and runs at
every control tick: sensing agents feed the edge fuzzy-consensus
operator, whose validated snapshot is checked by a non-bypassable
verifier that releases either a safe control action or a conservative
deterministic fallback. The \emph{slow path} is an event-triggered
supervisory loop in which a LangGraph-orchestrated LLM planner reasons
over consensus snapshots and proposes occasional supervisory actions;
crucially, these proposals re-enter the \emph{same} verifier before they
can affect actuation. A trustworthiness-and-traceability plane crosses
both paths.

\begin{figure*}[!tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.96\textwidth]{architecture_overview.pdf}
\caption{Two-path architecture. The deterministic \textbf{fast path}
(left) runs every tick: sensing $\rightarrow$ fuzzy consensus
$\rightarrow$ verifier $\rightarrow$ safe action or fallback. The sparse
\textbf{slow path} (right) is an event-triggered LangGraph/Gemma planner
whose proposals must clear the same non-bypassable verifier. A
traceability plane records all decisions.}
\label{fig:architecture}
\end{figure*}

\paragraph{Physical and sensing layer}
Sensor agents attached to racks, hosts, and power-distribution
interfaces emit per-round observations of thermal, power, and workload
variables, in line with hierarchical data-centre monitoring
practice~\cite{jiang2019,yan2021}. Actuator interfaces (fan speed,
cooling setpoints) close the physical loop.

\paragraph{Edge consensus layer}
Edge moderating agents collect the reports of one zone and execute the
FPR--OWA lightweight consensus operator
(Section~\ref{sec:fprowa}), producing a zone state estimate together
with diagnostic metadata: per-sensor reliability scores, anomaly flags,
and an aggregate confidence value. The result is packaged as a
\emph{consensus snapshot}, the only sensing artefact visible to the
upper layers.

\paragraph{Zone verification and coordination layer}
Zone verifier agents monitor the consensus process over time: they
compute consensus degree, per-agent proximity, and temporal
consistency, and trigger the adaptive feedback actions of
Section~\ref{sec:crp} (re-sampling, down-weighting, delayed inclusion,
persistent-anomaly flagging) when agreement degrades. This layer is the
sensor-agent analogue of the moderator in consensus-reaching
models~\cite{cabrerizo2009,herreraviedma2007}.

\paragraph{Agentic orchestration and control layer (slow path)}
A LangGraph-orchestrated LLM planner agent reasons over consensus
snapshots under a ReAct-style scheme~\cite{yao2023react} and proposes
structured \emph{supervisory} control actions. This layer is invoked
sparsely and asynchronously---triggered by low confidence, anomaly
flags, or a minimum inter-invocation interval---rather than on every
tick, because (as Section~\ref{sec:results} shows) a single LLM call
costs seconds and frequent invocation degrades real-time responsiveness
without improving estimation. Every proposal, whenever it is generated,
is checked by the same deterministic verifier of the fast path, which
accepts it, rejects it, or returns machine-readable feedback that drives
optional Reflexion-style self-refinement~\cite{shinn2023reflexion} for
at most $K$ cycles (Section~\ref{sec:agentic}).

\paragraph{Facility operation layer}
A facility arbiter resolves cross-zone trade-offs (thermal risk versus
energy cost, workload placement), consistent with the local--global
decomposition of autonomic data-centre
management~\cite{das2008,tesauro2004}, and exposes dashboards for human
oversight and escalation.

\paragraph{Trustworthiness and traceability plane}
Crossing all layers, a traceability service records consensus
snapshots, reliability vectors, planner prompts and outputs, verifier
decisions with reasons, refinement cycles, and safety counters,
yielding an auditable, replayable account of every control decision
(P5).

\subsection{System Model}
\label{sec:sysmodel}

Let $\mathcal{Z}$ be the set of zones. Zone $z\in\mathcal{Z}$ contains
$n_z$ sensing agents indexed by $E_z=\{1,\dots,n_z\}$. Time is
discretised into decision rounds $t=1,2,\dots$ aligned with the
telemetry period. At round $t$, agent $i\in E_z$ reports a measurement
\begin{equation}
s_i(t) \;=\; x_z(t) \;+\; \varepsilon_i(t) \;+\; a_i(t),
\label{eq:obsmodel}
\end{equation}
where $x_z(t)$ is the latent zone state (in the reference use case, a
zone thermal variable), $\varepsilon_i(t)$ is zero-mean sensing noise,
and $a_i(t)$ is an attack term, zero for honest reports. The exposition
uses a scalar state for clarity; the operators extend componentwise to
state vectors in $\R^d$ combining thermal, power, and workload
features.

Communication between sensing and edge agents is local, frequent, and
loss-prone; messages may be delayed, dropped, or duplicated, in the
spirit of lightweight agreement under lossy
networks~\cite{zhang2021approx}. Zone-to-facility communication is less
frequent and carries only consensus snapshots and verified control
intents. No global clock or reliable transport is assumed; each
snapshot carries a timestamp, and staleness is policed by the verifier
(Section~\ref{sec:verifier}).

\subsection{Fault and Attack Model}
\label{sec:faultmodel}

We consider a deception adversary who compromises a fraction
$\beta_a\in[0,1)$ of the sensing agents of a zone and manipulates their
reports through the attack term $a_i(t)$
in~\eqref{eq:obsmodel}. Table~\ref{tab:attacks} lists the attack
taxonomy, chosen to cover the canonical false-data-injection patterns
studied in CPS security~\cite{padhan2022} as they manifest in
slow-dynamics thermal telemetry. Benign faults (missing and delayed
reports, transient noise bursts) are handled by the same machinery,
since the consensus layer makes no attempt to distinguish malice from
malfunction: both surface as unreliable reports.

behaviour---a guarantee that ledger consensus buys at a cost

attack magnitude, while flagging the implicated reporters for the
feedback loop of Section~\ref{sec:crp}.

\section{Lightweight Consensus Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

This section formalises the consensus layer: the per-round FPR--OWA
operator executed by edge agents (Section~\ref{sec:fprowa}) and the
zone-level consensus-reaching loop that monitors and repairs agreement
over time (Section~\ref{sec:crp}). The design transfers, to sensor
agents, four ingredients of soft-consensus GDM: graded reliability in
place of expert importance, fuzzy preference relations in place of
pairwise expert judgements, quantifier-guided dominance in place of
selection degrees, and feedback in place of moderator advice.

\subsection{Reliability Scoring}
\label{sec:reliability}

Let $\mathbf{s}(t)=(s_1(t),\dots,s_{n_z}(t))$ be the report vector of
zone $z$ at round $t$ (zone subscripts are omitted where unambiguous).
A robust centre and scale are computed first:
\begin{equation}
m(t)=\operatorname{median}_i\, s_i(t),
\qquad
\hat{\sigma}(t)=1.4826\cdot\operatorname{MAD}_i\, s_i(t)+\epsilon,
\label{eq:centerscale}
\end{equation}
where $\operatorname{MAD}$ is the median absolute deviation, the
constant $1.4826$ makes $\hat\sigma$ consistent with the standard
deviation under Gaussian noise, and $\epsilon>0$ prevents degeneracy
when reports coincide. Each agent then receives a reliability score
\begin{equation}
\rel_i(t)\;=\;\exp\!\left(-\,\frac{\lvert s_i(t)-m(t)\rvert}
{\hat{\sigma}(t)}\right)\;\in\;(0,1].
\label{eq:reliability}
\end{equation}
Reliability decays exponentially with robust-standardised distance from
the group centre: agents near the consensus of their peers score close
to one, while outliers---whether noisy or attacked---are discounted
smoothly rather than excluded by a hard threshold. This is the
sensor-agent analogue of the graded importance weights of soft
consensus~\cite{kacprzyk1986,kacprzyk1988}.

\subsection{Fuzzy Preference Relation over Reporters}
\label{sec:fpr}

Pointwise reliability ignores the relational structure of trust: what
matters for ordering reporters is how much more credible one is than
another. Following the representation of graded pairwise judgements by
fuzzy preference relations~\cite{chiclana1998,herreraviedma2002}, we
build $P(t)=[p_{ij}(t)]\in[0,1]^{n_z\times n_z}$ with
\begin{equation}
p_{ij}(t)\;=\;\frac{1}{1+\exp\!\big(-\kappa\,(\rel_i(t)-\rel_j(t))\big)},
\qquad p_{ii}(t)=0.5,
\label{eq:fpr}
\end{equation}
where $\kappa>0$ controls the sharpness of the comparison. The relation
is additively reciprocal, $p_{ij}+p_{ji}=1$, and $p_{ij}>0.5$ reads
``report $i$ is more credible than report $j$''. The logistic link
makes the preference robust to small reliability differences (which map
to values near $0.5$) while saturating for clear-cut cases.

From $P(t)$, each agent receives a dominance degree
\begin{equation}
\delta_i(t)\;=\;\frac{1}{n_z-1}\sum_{j\neq i} p_{ij}(t)\;\in\;[0,1],
\label{eq:dominance}
\end{equation}
the average strength with which $i$ is preferred to its peers. This is
the operational counterpart of the quantifier-guided dominance degrees
used to exploit fuzzy preference relations in GDM selection
processes~\cite{chiclana1998,herreraviedma2002}; it induces the
credibility ordering used by the aggregation step.

\subsection{Quantifier-Guided OWA Aggregation}
\label{sec:fprowa}

Let $\sigma$ be the permutation of $E_z$ that sorts dominance degrees
in non-increasing order, $\delta_{\sigma(1)}\geq\cdots\geq
\delta_{\sigma(n_z)}$. Aggregation weights are derived from a regular
non-decreasing linguistic quantifier in the parametric family of
Zadeh~\cite{zadeh1983},
\begin{equation}
Q_{a,b}(r)=
\begin{cases}
0, & r<a,\\[2pt]
\dfrac{r-a}{b-a}, & a\leq r\leq b,\\[6pt]
1, & r>b,
\end{cases}
\qquad 0\le a<b\le 1,
\label{eq:quantifier}
\end{equation}
instantiated with $(a,b)=(0.3,0.8)$, the classical parametrisation of
the weak-majority quantifier ``most''~\cite{herrera1996,
herreraviedma2002}. The OWA weights of Yager~\cite{yager1988},
\begin{equation}
w_k \;=\; Q_{a,b}\!\left(\frac{k}{n_z}\right)-
Q_{a,b}\!\left(\frac{k-1}{n_z}\right),
\qquad k=1,\dots,n_z,
\label{eq:owaweights}
\end{equation}
are assigned along the credibility ordering so that the mass prescribed
by the quantifier falls on the most dominant reports, and the zone
estimate is
\begin{equation}
\hat{x}_z(t)\;=\;\sum_{k=1}^{n_z} w_k\, s_{\sigma(k)}(t).
\label{eq:estimate}
\end{equation}
The semantics is a fuzzy-majority fusion: the validated state is the
value supported by \emph{most of the credible} reporters, rather than
the unweighted average (vulnerable to coordinated bias) or the bare
median (which discards the graded structure of trust). Because ordering
is dominance-induced rather than value-induced, the operator behaves as
an induced OWA whose order-inducing variable is relational
credibility---the key difference from a direct OWA on sorted
measurement values, which we retain as an ablation baseline.

\subsection{Diagnostics and Consensus Snapshot}
\label{sec:snapshot}

Alongside the estimate, the edge agent emits diagnostics. Agents whose
reliability falls below an adaptive threshold are flagged as anomalous:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
A_z(t)&=\bigl\{\, i \in E_z :\ \rel_i(t) < \tau_{\rho}(t) \,\bigr\},\\
\tau_{\rho}(t)&=\max\!\bigl(\underline{\tau},\;
\operatorname{median}_i \rel_i(t) - 2\,\operatorname{std}_i
\rel_i(t)\bigr),
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:anomaly}
\end{equation}
with a floor $\underline{\tau}$ that keeps the threshold meaningful
when reliabilities are uniformly high. The zone confidence is the mean
reliability $\gamma_z(t)=\frac{1}{n_z}\sum_i \rel_i(t)$. The
\emph{consensus snapshot}
\begin{equation}
\chi_z(t)=\bigl(\hat{x}_z(t),\,\gamma_z(t),\,A_z(t),\,
\boldsymbol{\rel}(t),\,P(t),\,t\bigr)
\label{eq:snapshot}
\end{equation}
is the typed artefact passed upward; it carries enough evidence for the
verifier to police staleness and confidence, and for the traceability
plane to support post-hoc audit. The operator costs
$O(n_z^2)$ arithmetic per zone and round, dominated by the preference
relation; for zone-sized groups (tens of sensors) this is negligible on
edge-class hardware and involves no inter-zone communication, in
contrast with the $O(n^2)$ \emph{message} complexity of
PBFT-style quorums~\cite{castro1999}.

\subsection{Zone-Level Consensus Reaching and Adaptive Feedback}
\label{sec:crp}

A single aggregation round, however robust, cannot distinguish an agent
that is briefly noisy from one that is persistently deceptive, nor can
it recover information from agents that are systematically
down-weighted. The zone verifier therefore runs a consensus-reaching
process (CRP) over rounds, adapting the guidance-and-feedback scheme of
soft-consensus GDM~\cite{herreraviedma2002,herreraviedma2007,
cabrerizo2009,cabrerizo2010kbs} to sensor agents.

\paragraph{Consensus degree and proximity}
Pairwise similarity between reports is measured by soft coincidence,
\begin{equation}
s_{ij}(t)=\max\!\left(0,\;1-\frac{\lvert s_i(t)-s_j(t)\rvert}{M_z}\right),
\label{eq:similarity}
\end{equation}
with $M_z>0$ a zone normalisation constant (e.g., the admissible
operating range of the monitored variable). Agent proximity and the
zone consensus degree follow the three-level construction of
consensus models~\cite{cabrerizo2009}:
\begin{equation}
p_i(t)=\frac{1}{n_z-1}\sum_{j\neq i}s_{ij}(t),
\qquad
C_z(t)=\frac{1}{n_z}\sum_{i\in E_z}p_i(t).
\label{eq:consensusdegree}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Temporal consistency}
Mirroring the consistency control that prevents agreement from masking
self-contradiction~\cite{herreraviedma2007,cabrerizo2010kbs}, each agent
is also scored for coherence with its own recent behaviour. Let
$\tilde{s}_i(t)$ be a short-horizon prediction of agent $i$'s report
(in the simplest instantiation, an exponentially weighted moving
average of its past reports). The temporal consistency score is
\begin{equation}
k_i(t)=\max\!\left(0,\;1-\frac{\lvert s_i(t)-\tilde{s}_i(t)\rvert}
{M_z}\right),
\label{eq:consistency}
\end{equation}
and the combined per-agent reliability used by the CRP blends social
agreement and self-coherence,
\begin{equation}
r_i(t)=\alpha\, p_i(t) + (1-\alpha)\, k_i(t),
\qquad \alpha\in[0,1].
\label{eq:combined}
\end{equation}
Replay and freeze attacks are the motivating cases: a frozen sensor can
remain socially plausible for a while yet fails consistency once the
zone dynamics move, whereas a noisy-but-honest sensor fails proximity
transiently while staying self-consistent.

\paragraph{Acceptance and feedback}
Given thresholds $\tau_c$ (zone consensus) and $\tau_r$ (agent
reliability), the verifier proceeds as follows at each round:
\begin{enumerate}
\item compute $\chi_z(t)$ via
Sections~\ref{sec:reliability}--\ref{sec:snapshot}, and
$C_z(t)$, $r_i(t)$ via~\eqref{eq:consensusdegree}--\eqref{eq:combined};
\item if $C_z(t)\geq\tau_c$, accept and forward $\chi_z(t)$;
\item otherwise, build the feedback set
$F_z(t)=\{\,i : r_i(t)<\tau_r\,\}$ and apply to each $i\in F_z(t)$ one
or more corrective actions:
\emph{re-sampling} (request a fresh report),
\emph{temporary down-weighting} (attenuate $i$'s influence in the next
aggregation),
\emph{delayed inclusion} (exclude $i$ for one round), or a
\emph{persistent-anomaly flag} when $r_i$ remains below $\tau_r$ for
$L$ consecutive rounds, which escalates the agent to the facility layer
for inspection;
\item re-validate and forward the resulting snapshot, recording all
feedback actions in the trace.
\end{enumerate}
This is the sensor-agent analogue of moderator advice generation: the
process targets the participants most responsible for disagreement
instead of recomputing blindly over the whole
zone~\cite{cabrerizo2009,cabrerizo2010kbs}. The CRP costs the same
$O(n_z^2)$ per round as the operator itself and never blocks the
control loop: when consensus cannot be restored within a round, the
snapshot is forwarded with degraded confidence, and the confidence
gate of the verifier (Section~\ref{sec:verifier}) decides whether
control may proceed.

\section{Verifier-Gated Agentic Control}
\label{sec:agentic}

The control side of the architecture turns validated state into safe
actuation. It deliberately separates a flexible, explainable
\emph{proposer} from a rigid, deterministic \emph{gatekeeper}.

\subsection{Event-Triggered ReAct Planner Agent}
\label{sec:planner}

The planner is an LLM agent orchestrated with LangGraph and backed, in
our implementation, by a Gemma instruction-tuned model served behind a
standard inference endpoint. Operating under a ReAct-style
scheme~\cite{yao2023react}, it receives the consensus snapshot
$\chi_z(t)$ (and, where relevant, recent history and the current
actuator state), reasons explicitly about the thermal and energy
situation of the zone, and emits a \emph{structured} supervisory action
\begin{equation}
u_z(t)=\bigl(\Delta f,\ \theta_{\mathrm{set}},\
\textit{rationale},\ \textit{used\_consensus}\bigr),
\label{eq:action}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta f$ is a fan-speed adjustment, $\theta_{\mathrm{set}}$ a
cooling setpoint, \textit{rationale} a natural-language justification
retained for audit, and \textit{used\_consensus} a declaration that the
proposal was grounded in the validated snapshot rather than in raw or
remembered values. Output is constrained to a typed JSON schema;
malformed or schema-violating generations do not stall the loop---they
trigger the deterministic fallback (below) and are recorded as handled
events, not crashes.

Crucially, the planner is \emph{not} placed on the per-tick critical
path. It is invoked by an event-triggered policy parameterised by a
confidence threshold, an anomaly-count threshold, a temperature margin,
and a minimum inter-invocation interval; between triggers the fast path
runs alone. The planner is also model-agnostic: any
instruction-following LLM can fill the role, and---by construction---the
architecture's \emph{safety} properties depend on the verifier, not on
the planner behaving well.

\subsection{Deterministic Verifier Agent}
\label{sec:verifier}

The verifier $V$ is a deterministic rule-based agent---no learned
components---that evaluates every proposed action against a declarative
policy. Its checks fall into two groups.

\emph{Process checks} establish that the proposal is procedurally
trustworthy: the planner declared grounding in the consensus snapshot;
the snapshot is fresh, $t_{\mathrm{now}}-t \leq \tau_{\mathrm{age}}$;
and the zone confidence clears the floor,
$\gamma_z(t)\geq\tau_{\gamma}$.

\emph{Safety checks} establish that the action is physically
admissible: actuator values within configured bounds; slew rates within
limits; and the predicted post-action zone temperature---obtained from
a conservative first-order thermal model---compliant with the
service-level threshold $T_{\mathrm{SLA}}$, with a stricter emergency
rule activating at $T_{\mathrm{emerg}}$.

The verdict is
\begin{equation}
V\bigl(u_z(t),\,\chi_z(t),\,\textit{actuators}\bigr)\in
\{\textsc{accept},\textsc{reject}\}
\quad\text{with reasons } R(t),
\label{eq:verdict}
\end{equation}
where $R(t)$ is a machine-readable list of violated checks. Only
\textsc{accept}ed actions reach the actuator interfaces.

\paragraph{Non-bypassability}
The actuation path is wired exclusively through $V$: planner outputs
have no side effects, and the actuator interface consumes only
verifier-signed actions. Liveness is preserved by a deterministic
fallback policy (a conservative rule-based controller) that takes over
when no proposal is accepted within the refinement budget, so safety
never depends on the LLM behaving well and control never stalls on the
LLM behaving badly.

\subsection{Self-Refinement Cycles}
\label{sec:refinement}

On \textsc{reject}, the reasons $R(t)$ are fed back to the planner,
which may revise its proposal---a Reflexion-style verbal-feedback
loop~\cite{shinn2023reflexion}---for at most $K$ cycles:
\begin{equation}
u_z^{(c+1)}(t)=\pi_{\mathrm{LLM}}\bigl(\chi_z(t),\,R^{(c)}(t)\bigr),
\qquad c=0,\dots,K-1.
\label{eq:refinement}
\end{equation}
Refinement converts the verifier from a pure filter into a teacher: the
planner learns, within the episode, which constraint it violated and
how to satisfy it, without any weight update. The evaluation
(Section~\ref{sec:setup}) treats the planner variants---deterministic
policy only, ReAct without verifier, ReAct with verifier, ReAct with
verifier and self-refinement---as ablation conditions for RQ3.

\subsection{Trustworthiness and Traceability}
\label{sec:trace}

Every round appends to the execution trace: the consensus snapshot and
its diagnostics, CRP feedback actions, planner prompt and raw output,
verifier verdict and reasons, refinement cycles, the executed action,
and timing and token accounting. From these traces the framework
derives the safety indicators used in the evaluation---rates of unsafe
actions \emph{proposed}, \emph{blocked}, and (the critical one)
\emph{executed}, acceptance rates, and refinement-cycle counts---as
well as latency and overhead profiles. The trace design follows the
same auditability rationale as structured reasoning
traces~\cite{wei2022cot} and agreement-based stability signals over
sampled outputs~\cite{wang2023selfconsistency}, but anchors trust in
recorded \emph{decisions} rather than in the persuasiveness of
generated explanations.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

The architecture is evaluated on a real-data cyber--physical simulation:
a hierarchical IoT/MAS data-centre testbed in which sensing agents,
edge moderating agents, and the verifier-gated orchestrator operate over
telemetry drawn from audited public datasets. The study is a
simulation, not a physical deployment; this scoping is intentional and
its consequences are discussed in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\subsection{Data and Their Audited Roles}
\label{sec:setup_data}

Dataset names were treated as hypotheses and validated by an automated
audit before use; each dataset is admitted only for the role its schema
supports (Table~\ref{tab:datasets}). Thermal calibration---the
hot-corridor temperature signal that the consensus layer
estimates---comes from the Kaggle \emph{Data Centre Warm/Hot Corridor
Temperature Prediction} dataset (fields \texttt{TLHC},
\texttt{T\_MEAS-*}, \texttt{P\_cu-*}). The ENACT edge--cloud continuum
telemetry (Zenodo record 18920397; \texttt{CPU\%}, \texttt{MEM},
\texttt{Energy\,W}) is consumed as a power/workload proxy in the
simulation ($240$ rows per scenario, recorded in each trace's metadata);
the audit marks it usable for power but \emph{not} for temperature, so it
is never used as a thermal signal. Because the reported error metrics
evaluate the thermal estimate, ENACT enters as simulation context rather
than as an evaluated target. Anomaly and deception \emph{timing} is
taken from CuCD-ID, a CubeSat cybersecurity intrusion-detection dataset
(Mendeley Data, CC~BY~4.0); per the audit's mapping rule, this
cyber dataset contributes only the temporal placement of
anomaly/attack episodes and is never interpreted as a thermal
measurement. Google Cluster Data, Building Data Genome Project~2, and
CICIoT2023 were considered as candidates/substitutes but were not the
datasets used in the final run. The Kaggle thermal values appear
standardised; all error metrics are therefore reported in dataset units
unless inverse-scaling metadata is available.

\subsection{Scenario Design}
\label{sec:setup_scenarios}

The full campaign comprises $1{,}890$ scenarios producing $11{,}340$
per-method metric rows, $30{,}240$ agentic-safety rows, and $41{,}580$
latency rows. Scenarios vary seven deception/anomaly attack types
(Table~\ref{tab:attacks}: bias, drift, freeze, mixed, replay, scaling,
spike), the attacked-sensor fraction $\beta_a\in\{0.0,0.1,0.2,0.3,0.4\}$,
sensor noise levels $\{0.1,0.25,0.5\}$, zone sizes
$n_z\in\{5,9,13\}$, OWA parameters $(\alpha,\beta)\in\{(0.2,0.7),(0.3,0.8)\}$,
and three random seeds $\{11,42,101\}$. The thermal response follows a
first-order model with disturbance, and attacks are activated over a
bounded fraction of each episode rather than for its entire duration.

\subsection{Consensus Methods and Agentic Variants}
\label{sec:setup_methods}

Six aggregators are compared under identical conditions: arithmetic
\emph{mean}, \emph{trimmed mean}, \emph{Hampel-filter-then-mean},
\emph{median}, \emph{direct OWA} (value-ordered, same quantifier), and
the proposed \emph{FPR--OWA} (dominance-ordered, with reliability
diagnostics). Four agentic-control variants instantiate the safety
ablation: \emph{deterministic policy only}, \emph{ReAct without
verifier}, \emph{ReAct with verifier}, and \emph{ReAct with verifier and
self-refinement}. Two real-time supervision regimes are studied
separately (Section~\ref{sec:results_rq4}): an \emph{intensive} regime
that invokes the LLM aggressively, and an \emph{event-triggered} regime
with a restrictive policy (confidence threshold $0.35$, anomaly
threshold $3$, temperature margin $0.02$, minimum inter-invocation
interval $20$ ticks, no real-time refinement), each evaluated against
deadline budgets of $\{100,250,500,1000,5000\}$\,ms.

\subsection{Metrics}
\label{sec:setup_metrics}

Estimation quality is measured by mean absolute error (MAE), root mean
square error (RMSE), median absolute error, the 95th-percentile absolute
error (P95~AE), and maximum absolute error, all in dataset units, plus
the mean number of anomaly flags and the mean confidence emitted per
method. Safety is measured by the counts of unsafe actions
\emph{proposed}, \emph{blocked} by the verifier, and---decisively---%
\emph{executed}, together with accepted safe actions and LLM/verifier
call counts. Latency is reported per decision for the consensus
operators and per planner$+$verifier invocation for the agentic
variants; the real-time study additionally reports loop-latency
percentiles, LLM invocation and failure rates, deadline-miss rates, and
thermal-SLA violation rates.

\subsection{Implementation}
\label{sec:setup_impl}

The fast path is implemented in Python with vectorised NumPy operators.
The orchestrator is built on LangGraph with a Gemma planner served behind
an OpenAI-compatible endpoint; the verifier is a dependency-free rule
engine driven by a declarative SLA policy ($T_{\mathrm{SLA}}$,
$T_{\mathrm{emerg}}$, $\tau_{\mathrm{age}}$, $\tau_{\gamma}$, actuator
bounds and slew limits, refinement budget $K$). All runs are seeded and
every scenario emits a full execution trace, supporting exact replay and
the post-hoc derivation of all reported metrics from measured files
only.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We report measured results only. Throughout, ``best'' denotes the
minimum-error method for the stated slice; no aggregate is projected or
smoothed.

\subsection{RQ1: How the Consensus Operators Compare}
\label{sec:results_rq1}

Averaged over all $1{,}890$ scenarios, the six aggregators are close and
\emph{no method dominates} (Table~\ref{tab:consensus_perf}). Plain
\emph{mean} attains the best global MAE ($1.5366$) and RMSE ($1.6210$),
with trimmed mean and Hampel-mean within $0.01$; FPR--OWA has the highest
($1.6171$). We report this plainly: for unstructured global error, simple
robust averaging is hard to beat on this data. The tail, however,
reorders the ranking---on P95~AE and maximum absolute error,
\emph{direct OWA} ($2.3906$) and \emph{median} ($2.3952$) lead the mean
($2.4419$)---so quantifier-guided aggregation helps where it is designed
to, in the worst case, even when it trails on the average.

Method advantage is also attack-dependent (Fig.~\ref{fig:heatmap}):
FPR--OWA is the single best aggregator under \emph{bias}
($1.6468$ vs.\ mean $1.7763$), where a systematic offset sharpens the
reliability ranking; Hampel-mean leads under \emph{spike} ($1.4716$) and
\emph{mixed}; mean leads under drift, freeze, replay, and scaling; and at
the heaviest compromise ($\beta_a=0.4$) the \emph{median} becomes most
competitive ($1.6133$). Yet the dominant driver of accuracy is not the
operator but sensor redundancy (Table~\ref{tab:sensors}): moving from
$5$ to $13$ sensors per zone cuts mean MAE from $1.8268$ to $1.1522$
($37\%$), far more than any between-method gap. The answer to RQ1 is
therefore that estimation quality is governed first by redundancy and
attack regime, and only secondarily by the aggregator; the fast path
should match the aggregator to the threat rather than fix a single
``winner''.

\subsection{RQ2: The Diagnostic Value of FPR--OWA}
\label{sec:results_rq2}

If FPR--OWA does not minimise MAE, why retain it? Because it is the only
method in the comparison that produces a usable trust signal. The robust
baselines emit a degenerate confidence of $1.000$ and zero anomaly flags
by construction (Table~\ref{tab:consensus_perf}): they return a number
with no statement about how much to believe it. FPR--OWA instead reports
a mean confidence of $0.532$ and an average of $1.109$ anomaly flags per
scenario, both derived from the reliability scores and the fuzzy
preference relation. These are exactly the inputs the verifier consumes
in its process checks (Section~\ref{sec:verifier}) and the event-trigger
policy uses to decide when to escalate to the LLM
(Section~\ref{sec:planner}). The contribution of FPR--OWA is thus
\emph{diagnostic and architectural} rather than accuracy-maximising: it
makes the consensus layer self-aware enough to gate verification and
supervision. A safety architecture that must decide \emph{when it does
not trust its own estimate} cannot be built on an aggregator that always
claims full confidence.

\subsection{RQ3: Safety Enforcement by the Deterministic Verifier}
\label{sec:results_rq3}

The safety ablation is unambiguous (Table~\ref{tab:safety}). With the
verifier removed, the ReAct planner
proposes $4{,}409$ unsafe actions and \emph{all $4{,}409$ are executed}.
Inserting the deterministic verifier blocks every one of the $4{,}358$
unsafe proposals it sees, driving unsafe executions to \emph{zero} while
still accepting $3{,}202$ safe actions. The deterministic-policy-only
variant likewise executes no unsafe action (it proposes none), but it
forgoes agentic flexibility entirely. Adding self-refinement raises
accepted safe actions from $3{,}202$ to $4{,}341$---the planner repairs
many initially rejected proposals from verifier feedback---while keeping
unsafe executions at zero; the cost is more LLM and verifier calls
($13{,}468$ each vs.\ $7{,}560$) and roughly $1.8\times$ the per-decision
latency ($12.95$\,s vs.\ $7.15$\,s). The answer to RQ3 is therefore
clear: the verifier is necessary and sufficient to eliminate unsafe
execution in our campaign, and self-refinement is a worthwhile but
strictly supervisory add-on whose latency makes it unsuitable for the
hard-real-time path.

\subsection{RQ4: Real-Time Feasibility of LLM Supervision}
\label{sec:results_rq4}

Latency makes the case for keeping the LLM off the critical path. The
fuzzy-consensus operators are lightweight---mean consensus costs
$\approx 0.044$\,s and FPR--OWA $\approx 0.085$\,s per
decision---whereas a single planner$+$verifier decision costs
$7.0$--$7.2$\,s, and $12.9$\,s with refinement
(Fig.~\ref{fig:latency}). The bottleneck is unambiguously the LLM, and
no consensus method approaches it.

The two real-time regimes quantify the consequence
(Table~\ref{tab:realtime}). Under \emph{intensive} supervision the LLM is
invoked on $74.6\%$ of ticks, the mean loop latency is $5{,}194.7$\,ms,
and the deadline-miss rate is $0.746$---equal to the invocation rate,
because a $\sim$7\,s call overruns every tested budget whenever it fires.
Under the \emph{event-triggered} policy the invocation rate falls to
$3.96\%$, the mean loop latency to $285.4$\,ms, and the deadline-miss
rate to $0.0396$, a $94.7\%$ reduction at every deadline from $100$ to
$5{,}000$\,ms. Critically, estimation quality is \emph{unchanged} between
the regimes (MAE $1.5090$, RMSE $1.6128$ in both) and the thermal-SLA
violation rate is $0.0$ throughout: the thermal estimate is produced by
the fuzzy-consensus fast path, so calling the LLM more often buys no
accuracy. The event-triggered loop latency is sharply bimodal---a P95 of
$0.459$\,ms (fast-path ticks) against a P99 of $7{,}313$\,ms (rare
LLM-triggered ticks)---so the system is near-real-time not because the
LLM is fast (its mean call latency remains $\approx 7.2$\,s) but because
it is called rarely. A small fraction of LLM outputs are JSON-invalid
(failure rate $\approx 0.011$); these are absorbed by the fallback and
recorded as handled events, which supports the necessity of the
verifier-plus-fallback design rather than indicating a failure of the
method.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Why a non-dominant operator is still the right one}
The result that simple averaging wins on global MAE while FPR--OWA does
not is, we argue, the most honest and the most useful finding of the
study. It separates two questions that are easy to conflate: \emph{which
operator estimates best} and \emph{which operator supports safe
orchestration}. On this data the answers differ. Averaging is an
excellent estimator but a poor sentinel---it cannot tell the verifier or
the trigger policy when to doubt it. FPR--OWA trades a small amount of
average accuracy for a confidence and anomaly signal that the rest of the
architecture is built on. In a system whose defining requirement is
knowing when \emph{not} to act autonomously, that trade is the right one;
and because the fast path is free to pick the per-attack best aggregator,
the architecture need not sacrifice accuracy to obtain the diagnostics.

\paragraph{Three concerns, three mechanisms}
The campaign supports a clean division of labour. Estimation accuracy is
won by robust aggregation and, above all, by sensor redundancy; safety is
won by the deterministic verifier, which reduces executed unsafe actions
to zero independently of how the LLM behaves; responsiveness is won by
event-triggered supervision, which preserves accuracy and SLA compliance
while restoring real-time margins. Placing a single powerful LLM
``in the loop'' would have coupled these concerns and degraded the third
without improving the first. The two-path design keeps them decoupled.

\paragraph{The role of agentic reasoning}
Our evidence does not support the strongest framing of LLM-in-the-loop
control, and we do not advance it. Frequent LLM supervision did not
improve estimation and made the loop miss deadlines; the LLM's value is
as a sparse supervisory layer that can reason over consensus snapshots
when the fast path signals low confidence or anomalies, and whose
proposals are always subordinate to the verifier. Self-refinement fits
this role well: it recovers more safe actions from verifier feedback, but
its added latency confirms that it belongs in supervision, not on the
critical path.

\paragraph{Positioning with respect to blockchain--IoT coordination}
The measured tens-of-milliseconds agreement on the fast path, with no
replicated ledger, confirms the positioning of
Section~\ref{sec:positioning}: blockchain mechanisms are complementary
instruments for notarising validated snapshots, not competitors for the
per-tick estimation problem, and are therefore not an estimation
baseline here.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Several limitations bound the claims above. The thermal signal comes from
a single Kaggle hot-corridor dataset whose values appear standardised;
absent inverse-scaling metadata, errors are in dataset units and absolute
magnitudes should not be read as degrees Celsius. CuCD-ID is spacecraft
cybersecurity telemetry and is used \emph{only} for pattern-level
anomaly/deception timing, never as a thermal measurement; ENACT supplies
workload/power context only. The study is a CPS/MAS \emph{simulation},
not a physical deployment, so claims concern state validation and
decision support rather than closed-loop actuation on real hardware. LLM
latency ($\approx 7$\,s per call) is incompatible with hard-real-time
operation if the planner is invoked frequently; our event-triggered
policy mitigates but does not remove this constraint, and the system is
near-real-time only because the LLM is called rarely. A non-negligible
fraction of LLM outputs are JSON-invalid, which the fallback handles but
which underscores the dependence on the verifier-plus-fallback layer.
FPR--OWA does not dominate the baselines in global MAE, and the
verifier's guarantee is an \emph{operational} one over the tested
scenarios, not a formal proof of Byzantine resilience. Finally, the
results are obtained on specific datasets and a particular thermal model;
generalisation to other facilities, richer workloads, and live control
requires further validation. The architecture should not be read as
production-ready.

\section{Conclusions and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusions}

We presented an agentic lightweight fuzzy-consensus architecture for
resilient data-centre cyber--physical monitoring and control under
deception attacks, evaluated over $1{,}890$ real-data scenarios and two
real-time supervision regimes. The evidence argues for a two-path design:
the safety-critical fast path stays lightweight, deterministic,
fuzzy-consensus driven, and verifier-guarded, while LLM reasoning serves
as sparse supervisory intelligence. Three findings support this. No
aggregator dominates---averaging minimises global MAE while FPR--OWA,
though not the most accurate, uniquely supplies the confidence and
anomaly diagnostics that gate verification and supervision, and sensor
redundancy outweighs operator choice. The deterministic verifier reduces
executed unsafe actions to zero, whereas removing it lets every unsafe
proposal run. And event-triggered supervision preserves accuracy and
zero SLA violations while cutting deadline misses by roughly $95\%$
relative to intensive LLM use, which improves nothing but responsiveness
harms.

Future work should validate the architecture on physical data-centre
testbeds and richer, less standardised workloads; develop facility-level
arbitration policies across zones; extend the consistency model and study
trust dynamics over longer horizons; calibrate the event-trigger policy
and verifier thresholds against operational SLAs; and treat disagreement
patterns and persistent anomaly flags as first-class signals for
anomaly detection and predictive maintenance.
