\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

The architecture is evaluated on a real-data cyber--physical simulation:
a hierarchical IoT/MAS data-centre testbed in which sensing agents,
edge moderating agents, and the verifier-gated orchestrator operate over
telemetry drawn from audited public datasets. The study is a
simulation, not a physical deployment; this scoping is intentional and
its consequences are discussed in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\subsection{Data and Their Audited Roles}
\label{sec:setup_data}

Dataset names were treated as hypotheses and validated by an automated
audit before use; each dataset is admitted only for the role its schema
supports (Table~\ref{tab:datasets}). Thermal calibration---the
hot-corridor temperature signal that the consensus layer
estimates---comes from the Kaggle \emph{Data Centre Warm/Hot Corridor
Temperature Prediction} dataset (fields \texttt{TLHC},
\texttt{T\_MEAS-*}, \texttt{P\_cu-*}). Workload and power context comes
from the ENACT edge--cloud continuum telemetry (Zenodo record
18920397; \texttt{CPU\%}, \texttt{MEM}, \texttt{Energy\,W}); the audit
marks ENACT usable for power but \emph{not} for temperature, and it is
never used as a thermal signal. Anomaly and deception \emph{timing} is
taken from CuCD-ID, a CubeSat cybersecurity intrusion-detection dataset
(Mendeley Data, CC~BY~4.0); per the audit's mapping rule, this
cyber dataset contributes only the temporal placement of
anomaly/attack episodes and is never interpreted as a thermal
measurement. Google Cluster Data, Building Data Genome Project~2, and
CICIoT2023 were considered as candidates/substitutes but were not the
datasets used in the final run. The Kaggle thermal values appear
standardised; all error metrics are therefore reported in dataset units
unless inverse-scaling metadata is available.

\subsection{Scenario Design}
\label{sec:setup_scenarios}

The full campaign comprises $1{,}890$ scenarios producing $11{,}340$
per-method metric rows, $30{,}240$ agentic-safety rows, and $41{,}580$
latency rows. Scenarios vary seven deception/anomaly attack types
(Table~\ref{tab:attacks}: bias, drift, freeze, mixed, replay, scaling,
spike), the attacked-sensor fraction $\beta_a\in\{0.0,0.1,0.2,0.3,0.4\}$,
sensor noise levels $\{0.1,0.25,0.5\}$, zone sizes
$n_z\in\{5,9,13\}$, OWA parameters $(\alpha,\beta)\in\{(0.2,0.7),(0.3,0.8)\}$,
and three random seeds $\{11,42,101\}$. The thermal response follows a
first-order model with disturbance, and attacks are activated over a
bounded fraction of each episode rather than for its entire duration.

\subsection{Consensus Methods and Agentic Variants}
\label{sec:setup_methods}

Six aggregators are compared under identical conditions: arithmetic
\emph{mean}, \emph{trimmed mean}, \emph{Hampel-filter-then-mean},
\emph{median}, \emph{direct OWA} (value-ordered, same quantifier), and
the proposed \emph{FPR--OWA} (dominance-ordered, with reliability
diagnostics). Four agentic-control variants instantiate the safety
ablation: \emph{deterministic policy only}, \emph{ReAct without
verifier}, \emph{ReAct with verifier}, and \emph{ReAct with verifier and
self-refinement}. Two real-time supervision regimes are studied
separately (Section~\ref{sec:results_rq4}): an \emph{intensive} regime
that invokes the LLM aggressively, and an \emph{event-triggered} regime
with a restrictive policy (confidence threshold $0.35$, anomaly
threshold $3$, temperature margin $0.02$, minimum inter-invocation
interval $20$ ticks, no real-time refinement), each evaluated against
deadline budgets of $\{100,250,500,1000,5000\}$\,ms.

\subsection{Metrics}
\label{sec:setup_metrics}

Estimation quality is measured by mean absolute error (MAE), root mean
square error (RMSE), median absolute error, the 95th-percentile absolute
error (P95~AE), and maximum absolute error, all in dataset units, plus
the mean number of anomaly flags and the mean confidence emitted per
method. Safety is measured by the counts of unsafe actions
\emph{proposed}, \emph{blocked} by the verifier, and---decisively---%
\emph{executed}, together with accepted safe actions and LLM/verifier
call counts. Latency is reported per decision for the consensus
operators and per planner$+$verifier invocation for the agentic
variants; the real-time study additionally reports loop-latency
percentiles, LLM invocation and failure rates, deadline-miss rates, and
thermal-SLA violation rates.

\subsection{Implementation}
\label{sec:setup_impl}

The fast path is implemented in Python with vectorised NumPy operators.
The orchestrator is built on LangGraph with a Gemma planner served behind
an OpenAI-compatible endpoint; the verifier is a dependency-free rule
engine driven by a declarative SLA policy ($T_{\mathrm{SLA}}$,
$T_{\mathrm{emerg}}$, $\tau_{\mathrm{age}}$, $\tau_{\gamma}$, actuator
bounds and slew limits, refinement budget $K$). All runs are seeded and
every scenario emits a full execution trace, supporting exact replay and
the post-hoc derivation of all reported metrics from measured files
only.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We report measured results only. Throughout, ``best'' denotes the
minimum-error method for the stated slice; no aggregate is projected or
smoothed.

\subsection{RQ1: How the Consensus Operators Compare}
\label{sec:results_rq1}

Averaged over all $1{,}890$ scenarios, the six aggregators are close and
\emph{no method dominates} (Table~\ref{tab:consensus_perf},
Fig.~\ref{fig:mae_method}). Plain \emph{mean} attains the best global MAE
($1.5366$) and RMSE ($1.6210$), with trimmed mean and Hampel-mean within
$0.01$. FPR--OWA has the highest global MAE ($1.6171$). This ordering is
stable and we report it plainly: for unstructured global error, simple
robust averaging is hard to beat on this data.

Two qualifications make the picture more interesting. First, the tail
tells a different story from the mean: on P95~AE the ranking reorders,
with \emph{direct OWA} ($2.3906$) and \emph{median} ($2.3952$) ahead of
mean ($2.4419$), and on maximum absolute error median and direct OWA are
again strongest (Fig.~\ref{fig:p95_method}). Quantifier-guided
aggregation thus helps precisely where it is designed to---in the
worst-case tail---even when it trails on the average. Second, the
per-attack breakdown (Table~\ref{tab:per_attack},
Fig.~\ref{fig:heatmap}) shows method advantage is attack-dependent:
FPR--OWA is the single best aggregator under \emph{bias}
($1.6468$ vs.\ mean $1.7763$), where a systematic offset sharpens the
reliability ranking; Hampel-mean leads under \emph{spike} ($1.4716$) and
\emph{mixed}; and mean leads under drift, freeze, replay, and scaling.
As the attacked fraction grows to $\beta_a=0.4$, the \emph{median}
becomes the most competitive aggregator ($1.6133$), as expected when a
larger minority is compromised.

The dominant driver of accuracy, however, is not the operator but sensor
redundancy (Table~\ref{tab:sensors}, Fig.~\ref{fig:sensors}). Moving from
$5$ to $13$ sensors per zone lowers MAE for every method---for mean, from
$1.8268$ to $1.1522$, a $37\%$ reduction---a far larger effect than any
between-method gap. The actionable answer to RQ1 is therefore that
estimation quality is governed first by redundancy and attack regime,
and only secondarily by the choice of aggregator; the fast path should
select the aggregator to the threat (e.g.\ Hampel under spikes, median
under heavy compromise) rather than commit to a single ``winner''.

\subsection{RQ2: The Diagnostic Value of FPR--OWA}
\label{sec:results_rq2}

If FPR--OWA does not minimise MAE, why retain it? Because it is the only
method in the comparison that produces a usable trust signal. The robust
baselines emit a degenerate confidence of $1.000$ and zero anomaly flags
by construction (Table~\ref{tab:consensus_perf}): they return a number
with no statement about how much to believe it. FPR--OWA instead reports
a mean confidence of $0.532$ and an average of $1.109$ anomaly flags per
scenario, both derived from the reliability scores and the fuzzy
preference relation. These are exactly the inputs the verifier consumes
in its process checks (Section~\ref{sec:verifier}) and the event-trigger
policy uses to decide when to escalate to the LLM
(Section~\ref{sec:planner}). The contribution of FPR--OWA is thus
\emph{diagnostic and architectural} rather than accuracy-maximising: it
makes the consensus layer self-aware enough to gate verification and
supervision. A safety architecture that must decide \emph{when it does
not trust its own estimate} cannot be built on an aggregator that always
claims full confidence.

\subsection{RQ3: Safety Enforcement by the Deterministic Verifier}
\label{sec:results_rq3}

The safety ablation is unambiguous (Table~\ref{tab:safety},
Fig.~\ref{fig:safety}). With the verifier removed, the ReAct planner
proposes $4{,}409$ unsafe actions and \emph{all $4{,}409$ are executed}.
Inserting the deterministic verifier blocks every one of the $4{,}358$
unsafe proposals it sees, driving unsafe executions to \emph{zero} while
still accepting $3{,}202$ safe actions. The deterministic-policy-only
variant likewise executes no unsafe action (it proposes none), but it
forgoes agentic flexibility entirely. Adding self-refinement raises
accepted safe actions from $3{,}202$ to $4{,}341$---the planner repairs
many initially rejected proposals from verifier feedback---while keeping
unsafe executions at zero; the cost is more LLM and verifier calls
($13{,}468$ each vs.\ $7{,}560$) and roughly $1.8\times$ the per-decision
latency ($12.95$\,s vs.\ $7.15$\,s). The answer to RQ3 is therefore
clear: the verifier is necessary and sufficient to eliminate unsafe
execution in our campaign, and self-refinement is a worthwhile but
strictly supervisory add-on whose latency makes it unsuitable for the
hard-real-time path.

\subsection{RQ4: Real-Time Feasibility of LLM Supervision}
\label{sec:results_rq4}

Latency makes the case for keeping the LLM off the critical path. The
fuzzy-consensus operators are lightweight---mean consensus costs
$\approx 0.044$\,s and FPR--OWA $\approx 0.085$\,s per
decision---whereas a single planner$+$verifier decision costs
$7.0$--$7.2$\,s, and $12.9$\,s with refinement
(Fig.~\ref{fig:latency}). The bottleneck is unambiguously the LLM, and
no consensus method approaches it.

The two real-time regimes quantify the consequence
(Table~\ref{tab:realtime}, Fig.~\ref{fig:realtime}). Under
\emph{intensive} supervision the LLM is invoked on $74.6\%$ of ticks; the
mean loop latency is $5{,}194.7$\,ms and the deadline-miss rate is
$0.746$---equal to the invocation rate, because a $\sim$7\,s call
overruns every tested budget whenever it fires. Under the
\emph{event-triggered} policy the invocation rate falls to $3.96\%$, the
mean loop latency to $285.4$\,ms, and the deadline-miss rate to $0.0396$,
a $94.7\%$ reduction at every deadline from $100$ to $5{,}000$\,ms
(Table~\ref{tab:deadline}). Critically, estimation quality is
\emph{unchanged} between the regimes (MAE $1.5090$, RMSE $1.6128$ in
both) and the thermal-SLA violation rate is $0.0$ throughout: the
thermal estimate is produced by the fuzzy-consensus fast path, so
calling the LLM more often buys no accuracy. The event-triggered loop
latency is sharply bimodal---a P95 of $0.459$\,ms (fast-path ticks)
against a P99 of $7{,}313$\,ms (the rare LLM-triggered ticks),
Fig.~\ref{fig:rtdist}. The system becomes near-real-time not because the
LLM is fast---its mean call latency remains $\approx 7.2$\,s---but
because it is called rarely. A small fraction of LLM outputs are
JSON-invalid (failure rate $\approx 0.011$); these are absorbed by the
fallback and recorded as handled events, which is evidence for the
necessity of the verifier-plus-fallback design rather than a failure of
the method.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Why a non-dominant operator is still the right one}
The result that simple averaging wins on global MAE while FPR--OWA does
not is, we argue, the most honest and the most useful finding of the
study. It separates two questions that are easy to conflate: \emph{which
operator estimates best} and \emph{which operator supports safe
orchestration}. On this data the answers differ. Averaging is an
excellent estimator but a poor sentinel---it cannot tell the verifier or
the trigger policy when to doubt it. FPR--OWA trades a small amount of
average accuracy for a confidence and anomaly signal that the rest of the
architecture is built on. In a system whose defining requirement is
knowing when \emph{not} to act autonomously, that trade is the right one;
and because the fast path is free to pick the per-attack best aggregator,
the architecture need not sacrifice accuracy to obtain the diagnostics.

\paragraph{Three concerns, three mechanisms}
The campaign supports a clean division of labour. Estimation accuracy is
won by robust aggregation and, above all, by sensor redundancy; safety is
won by the deterministic verifier, which reduces executed unsafe actions
to zero independently of how the LLM behaves; responsiveness is won by
event-triggered supervision, which preserves accuracy and SLA compliance
while restoring real-time margins. Placing a single powerful LLM
``in the loop'' would have coupled these concerns and degraded the third
without improving the first. The two-path design keeps them decoupled.

\paragraph{The role of agentic reasoning}
Our evidence does not support the strongest framing of LLM-in-the-loop
control, and we do not advance it. Frequent LLM supervision did not
improve estimation and made the loop miss deadlines; the LLM's value is
as a sparse supervisory layer that can reason over consensus snapshots
when the fast path signals low confidence or anomalies, and whose
proposals are always subordinate to the verifier. Self-refinement fits
this role well: it recovers more safe actions from verifier feedback, but
its added latency confirms that it belongs in supervision, not on the
critical path.

\paragraph{Positioning with respect to blockchain--IoT coordination}
The measured latencies reinforce the qualitative positioning of
Section~\ref{sec:positioning}. The fast path agrees on operational state
in tens of milliseconds without any replicated ledger; blockchain
mechanisms remain complementary instruments for notarising validated
snapshots or settling cross-organisational records, not competitors for
the per-tick estimation problem. We did not, and on this evidence should
not, run a blockchain method as an estimation baseline.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Several limitations bound the claims above. The thermal signal comes from
a single Kaggle hot-corridor dataset whose values appear standardised;
absent inverse-scaling metadata, errors are in dataset units and absolute
magnitudes should not be read as degrees Celsius. CuCD-ID is spacecraft
cybersecurity telemetry and is used \emph{only} for pattern-level
anomaly/deception timing, never as a thermal measurement; ENACT supplies
workload/power context only. The study is a CPS/MAS \emph{simulation},
not a physical deployment, so claims concern state validation and
decision support rather than closed-loop actuation on real hardware. LLM
latency ($\approx 7$\,s per call) is incompatible with hard-real-time
operation if the planner is invoked frequently; our event-triggered
policy mitigates but does not remove this constraint, and the system is
near-real-time only because the LLM is called rarely. A non-negligible
fraction of LLM outputs are JSON-invalid, which the fallback handles but
which underscores the dependence on the verifier-plus-fallback layer.
FPR--OWA does not dominate the baselines in global MAE, and the
verifier's guarantee is an \emph{operational} one over the tested
scenarios, not a formal proof of Byzantine resilience. Finally, the
results are obtained on specific datasets and a particular thermal model;
generalisation to other facilities, richer workloads, and live control
requires further validation. The architecture should not be read as
production-ready.

\section{Conclusions and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusions}

We presented an agentic lightweight fuzzy-consensus architecture for
resilient monitoring and control of data-centre cyber--physical systems
under deception and anomaly attacks, and evaluated it on a real-data
simulation spanning $1{,}890$ scenarios and two real-time supervision
regimes. The evidence supports a deliberately measured set of claims. No
consensus operator dominates: simple robust averaging minimises global
MAE, FPR--OWA wins under systematic bias and, uniquely, supplies the
confidence and anomaly diagnostics on which safe orchestration depends,
and sensor redundancy outweighs the choice of operator. The deterministic
verifier is essential---it reduces executed unsafe actions to zero, where
removing it lets every unsafe proposal run---and self-refinement raises
accepted safe actions at a latency cost that confines it to supervision.
Intensive LLM-in-the-loop supervision does not improve estimation and
breaks real-time deadlines, whereas event-triggered supervision preserves
accuracy and zero SLA violations while cutting deadline misses by roughly
$95\%$. Taken together, these results argue for an architecture in which
the safety-critical path stays lightweight, deterministic,
fuzzy-consensus driven, and verifier-guarded, while LLM-based agentic
reasoning serves as sparse supervisory intelligence.

Future work should validate the architecture on physical data-centre
testbeds and richer, less standardised workloads; develop facility-level
arbitration policies across zones; extend the consistency model and study
trust dynamics over longer horizons; calibrate the event-trigger policy
and verifier thresholds against operational SLAs; and treat disagreement
patterns and persistent anomaly flags as first-class signals for
anomaly detection and predictive maintenance.
